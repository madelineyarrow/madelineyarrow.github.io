## My Good Friend, the Coffee Mug

My good friend, the coffee mug.

At least, I hope we're good friends. I kiss him every morning.

What an awkward way to begin every day: with someone else's lips on you, and not even liking her.

Could you imagine?

---

As I began _[She Dressed for No One](/books/she-dressed-for-no-one/)_, I knew I wanted to tell a very close, very intimate story.

And what could be more intimate than <a href="/links/only-two-characters/" data-goatcounter-click="only-two-characters">only two characters</a>?

So I made that a constraint.

Which meant no supporting cast to carry information. No side conversations to reveal what either of them was thinking. No subplot to step away into when the tension became uncomfortable.

Everything had to stay with them.

And when everything has to stay with two people, the ordinary world around them starts to matter more.

Not just what they touch, but how they touch it. What they notice. What they avoid. What they hear when the room goes quiet. What warmth feels like in their hands.

Chapter One opens with:

> He pressed the hotel door until it sealed, feeling the latch catch with a metallic chink, choosing the quiet click over the hollow slam it might have made. He turned the bolt slowly and raised the swing bar. The room waited.

He could have simply shut the door.

Instead, he chooses the quiet click over the hollow slam.

What does that small choice tell us about him? About how he's feeling? About what he wants from the room now that he's alone inside it?

That's what interests me about ordinary things in a story like this. They can reveal something without ever explaining it.

More importantly, they can make us wonder.

Why this choice? Why this detail? Why does it matter?

The questions want answers.

And without quite realizing it, we're already being introduced to someone.

The story begins to unfold.

And sometimes the world gets to answer back.

> A glint of light caught his eye in the bathroom mirror. For a moment he thought it might be a scratch in the glass, but then he saw it—a single silver hair. It mocked him.
>
> He leaned toward the mirror, bracing his palms on the cold counter. The hair caught the light again—one defiant strand in a head still pretending at youth. He ran a hand slowly through his hair, watching it fall back into place as if nothing had changed.

The hair isn't mocking him, of course.

He is.

But giving the feeling to the hair lets it exist outside him for a moment.

A door can hold tension. A silver hair can hold everything the mirror leaves out. And yes, a coffee mug can hold warmth.

Suddenly the room is doing more than surrounding the character.

It's telling us who he is.
