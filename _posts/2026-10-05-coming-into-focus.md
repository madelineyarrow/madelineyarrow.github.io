## Coming into Focus

Family game night. Someone brings out a new box.

"Let's try this one."

Then comes the rulebook.

Suddenly everyone is a little less enthusiastic.

Maybe we'll just play something we already know.

Stories have the same problem. A reader wants to begin before they understand all the rules.

Oh, you want to read this book?

Wonderful.

Here's a small <a href="/links/pile-of-exposition/" data-goatcounter-click="pile-of-exposition">pile of exposition</a> to sift through first.

And introducing a character can go wrong in much the same way.

It reminds me a little of a bad first date.

The kind that slowly turns into a job interview.

Where did you grow up? What do you do? Do you have brothers or sisters? What do you like to do for fun?

Twenty minutes later, you know an awful lot about the person sitting across from you.

And somehow you still haven't met them.

A first chapter has quite a bit to do.

Someone has to come into focus. The story has to find its tone. Something has to make us want to turn the page.

And somewhere beneath all of that, the reader is quietly asking two questions:

Why am I here?

And why should I care?

Instead of explaining someone, let them come into focus slowly.

A choice here. A habit there. The way something is touched. A thing noticed that someone else might miss.

A reader doesn't need to know everything at once.

They just need enough to start forming a picture.

> The television came to life, light washing over the room. The voices changed with every channel—news, weather, laughter tracks—but all of it sounded the same. Dissatisfied, he set the remote down and sat for a moment in the flicker, waiting for something that didn’t come.
>
> He untucked the top sheet on one of the beds, not understanding how anyone could sleep pinned beneath it. Extra pillows came over from the other bed, tomorrow’s clothes laid out—a small favor to the version of himself who would wake late.

And from those small things, we can start to understand someone.

Not completely.

But enough to begin making guesses.

Enough to notice a pattern.

Enough to wonder whether the next small thing will confirm what we think we see—or change it.

And somewhere along the way, without anyone ever stopping to explain him to us, he comes into focus.
